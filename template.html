Bubble dynamics: a single gas bubble in a sound field

This program follows one gas bubble in water, or in another liquid, while a sound wave drives it. It solves the Keller-Miksis equation, which is the Rayleigh-Plesset equation with a correction for the compressibility of the liquid. Figure 1 shows how the radius changes with time. Figure 2 shows, for bubbles of different sizes, the sound pressure at which a bubble starts to cavitate inertially. Choose an example or enter your own values and press Compute.

About the data. This is a theoretical simulation built from published equations. The checks compare it with known analytical results. No measured data, and no data from any research centre, were used.

What it is for

In ultrasonic and megasonic cleaning, small gas bubbles in the liquid are driven by the sound field. At low sound pressure a bubble only oscillates gently around its starting size. Above a threshold it grows to several times its size and then collapses violently. This is inertial cavitation. The collapse cleans surfaces, but it can also damage fine structures, which is why the threshold matters in wafer cleaning.

How to read the results

In Figure 1, a gently oscillating bubble stays close to R/R0 = 1. An inertial bubble grows well beyond 2, collapses to a small fraction of its starting radius and then rebounds. The lower panel shows the sound pressure. The program starts with the low-pressure half of the cycle, so the bubble first grows.

In Figure 2, the solid line is the sound pressure at which a bubble of radius R0 first reaches twice its starting radius within the chosen number of cycles. Growing to 2 R0 is a simple, widely used rule of thumb for the onset of inertial cavitation. It is a convention, not a sharp physical boundary. The line dips near the radius that is in resonance with the sound: those bubbles respond most strongly. Smaller bubbles are held together by surface tension, and larger bubbles are too slow to follow the sound at this frequency. Smaller dips can appear at radii whose own resonance is a multiple of the driving frequency. The dashed line is the static Blake threshold. It is the right limit when the sound is much slower than the bubble's own oscillation.

Figure 2 also shows the threshold for a second gas model, for comparison: with heat exchange (blue) if the form uses adiabatic or isothermal gas, and adiabatic gas (grey) if it uses heat exchange. With heat exchange, small bubbles are nearly isothermal. Their gas is softer, so the threshold is lower than for adiabatic gas. The resonance moves to a smaller radius, and near resonance the thermal damping raises the lowest point of the curve. Each line has 22 points, so the narrow dips near resonance are only roughly resolved.

Model

Keller-Miksis equation for the bubble radius R(t), with Ṙ = dR/dt:

(1 − Ṙ/c) R R̈ + (3/2)(1 − Ṙ/(3c)) Ṙ² = (1 + Ṙ/c)(p_B − p_∞)/ρ + (R/(ρc)) dp_B/dt

p_B = p_g0 (R0/R)^(3κ) + p_v − 2σ/R − 4μṘ/R      pressure in the liquid at the bubble wall
p_g0 = p0 + 2σ/R0 − p_v                            gas pressure in the bubble at rest
p_∞ = p0 − p_A sin(2π f t)                         ambient pressure plus the sound field

For c → ∞ this becomes the Rayleigh-Plesset equation. The driving pressure is taken at the bubble's position at time t, which neglects the travel time R/c across the bubble. The equation is solved with an adaptive Dormand-Prince Runge-Kutta method with a relative tolerance of 10−9.

Linear resonance frequency and static Blake threshold (isothermal gas):

f_0 = (1 / (2π R0)) sqrt((3κ p_g0 − 2σ/R0) / ρ)

R_c = R0 sqrt(3 p_g0 R0 / (2σ))                    critical radius
p_A,Blake = p0 − p_v + 4σ / (3 R_c)                 sound amplitude needed

Heat exchange between the gas and the liquid. A small bubble, or a slow sound, leaves time for heat to flow between the gas and the liquid, and the gas stays close to the liquid temperature (isothermal, κ = 1). A large bubble, or a fast sound, leaves no time, and the gas behaves adiabatically (κ = γ = 1.4 for air). In between, part of the work done on the gas is lost as heat in every cycle, which damps the oscillation. The linear theory of Prosperetti, Crum and Commander (1988) gives both effects through one complex number Φ: 

Φ = 3γ / (1 − 3(γ − 1) i χ [ (i/χ)^(1/2) coth (i/χ)^(1/2) − 1 ])
χ = D / (ω R0²)                                     ω = 2π f, D = thermal diffusivity of the gas

κ = Re(Φ) / 3                                       effective polytropic exponent
μ_th = p_g0 Im(Φ) / (4ω)                            thermal damping, added to the viscosity μ

The diffusivity of air is 2.074 × 10−5 m²/s at 1 atm and 20 °C (Çengel and Cimbala). It is scaled with the inverse of the gas density, so it is taken at the pressure inside the bubble, p0 + 2σ/R0. With the option "with heat exchange", κ and μ_th are computed for each bubble radius at the chosen frequency, both in Figure 1 and in Figure 2, and the linear resonance frequency uses this κ. The factor 4 in μ_th follows from the linear gas pressure, p_g ≈ p_g0 (1 − Φ x) for R = R0 (1 + x): its imaginary part acts like a viscous pressure 4 μ_th Ṙ / R.

Warm water has a higher vapour pressure and a lower surface tension, so there is less gas in the bubble at rest and less to hold it together. The Blake threshold falls, from 142 kPa at 20 °C to 122 kPa at 60 °C for a 1 µm bubble. 2-propanol has less than a third of the surface tension of water, and the threshold for a 1 µm bubble is 104 kPa. Its viscosity is twice that of water, which damps the oscillation more strongly.

Limitations

  • One spherical bubble in an unbounded liquid. Near a wall, for example a wafer, a collapsing bubble becomes non-spherical and can form a liquid jet. That is not modelled.
  • The gas follows a polytropic law. With the option "with heat exchange", the exponent κ and an extra thermal damping come from the linear theory of Prosperetti, Crum and Commander (1988) for the chosen radius and frequency. That theory holds for small oscillations, so in a violent collapse it is only a rough guide. There is no gas diffusion into or out of the bubble, so slow growth over many cycles (rectified diffusion) is missing.
  • The Keller-Miksis equation is first order in the Mach number Ṙ/c. When the wall speed during a collapse comes close to the speed of sound, the details of the collapse are rough.
  • The liquid properties are constant and the sound field is a pure sine wave. The heat exchange uses the properties of air at 20 °C for every liquid and temperature, and the gas does not take up vapour from the liquid.
